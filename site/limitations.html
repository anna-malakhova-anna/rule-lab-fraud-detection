What flatters these numbers, and what they leave out

What flatters these results

  • I wrote the fraud. The rules look for exactly the shapes I planted, so detection rates in the 90s say more about my generator than about any real bank. Real fraud adapts once it starts getting caught.
  • Synthetic bust-outs are clean. Every one draws the full line within days of an increase, which R12 catches almost perfectly. Real bust-outs are messier and slower.
  • The final grade uses the full answer key. Tuning only sees labels known by month 12, but the headline comparison uses every label, including cases a real bank would never confirm.
  • Generator shortcuts can leak into results. I've found three. Bust-out money sent back to the owner's own account made R5 look like a bust-out detector (fixed). ACH fraud cash-outs to the owner's account let R5 catch part of the month-12 pattern by luck (documented, not removed). And some existing businesses that turned to mule or deposit fraud were active months before their account opened, which the case-summary check caught (fixed).

What makes them uncertain

  • Small samples. The test months hold 80 cases across seven fraud types, 6 to 22 each. One case moves a fraud type's numbers a lot; the headline carries bootstrap ranges for that reason.
  • Invented costs. Cutoffs are chosen with costs I made up (config/backtest.yaml): $30 a review, $150 per good business flagged, $1,000 per delayed payroll, $300 per good applicant declined. Different costs pick different cutoffs.
  • Vendors that know the answer. The invented vendors' scores are drawn from the planted truth with noise. A real vendor's signal is murkier, and whether it adds anything depends on how much the in-house rules already cover. Each vendor's added dollars also rest on one to three cases, so its buy or don't-buy call is fragile.
  • Fraudsters adapt once. After the instant-payout launch, fraudsters switch to instant and split cash-outs under the cap, but they don't yet adapt to the launch controls themselves.
  • The fast lane's cap is untested against splitting. Fraudsters split instant payouts under that cap; nothing here makes them split payments under the fast lane's cap too. A cap on two days' outflow would resist it.
  • The summary check proves traceability, not meaning. A sentence with no numbers that cites a real field passes whatever it says. Template summaries can't do that; a language-model writer could.
  • A simple queue. The Risk Ops simulation assumes the same capacity every day, one decision per case, no escalation tier, and invented analyst error rates (10% of fraud cleared by mistake, 2% of good cases flagged).
  • Simplified mechanics. There are no account balances or overdrafts, an RFI is assumed to stop half the fraud, and a hold is assumed to stop all of it.
  • A thin tuning set for credit. Bust-out labels arrive about five months after the money leaves, so tuning in month 12 saw only a handful of confirmed bust-outs.

What isn't modelled

  • Fraud that adapts to the rules: a ring buying aged shelf companies to dodge R6's decline, or money moved out through fake payroll runs to dodge R1.
  • Customer reactions to friction (churn), chargebacks, and the card rail beyond aggregate spend.
